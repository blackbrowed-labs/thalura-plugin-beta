# Thinking & Dilemma Methods

Methods that provoke cognitive conflict, test intuitions, and sharpen conceptual thinking — the heart of Philosophy didactics, transferable to ethical and thematic discussions in English.

---

## Thought Experiments (Gedankenexperimente)

**Grades:** 7–S4
**Phase:** Einstieg, Erarbeitung
**Time:** 15–30 min

**Procedure:**
1. Present a thought experiment: a vivid hypothetical scenario designed to test a principle or intuition. Classic examples: Trolley Problem, Ship of Theseus, Brain in a Vat, Ring of Gyges, Mary's Room.
2. Students write their **immediate intuitive response** (1–2 min, no discussion yet).
3. Share responses — map the spread of opinions (tally, show of hands, or Opinion Line).
4. Structured discussion: Why did you answer that way? What principle underlies your intuition?
5. Introduce variations that shift the scenario: Does changing detail X change your answer? Why?
6. Connect to philosophical theory: Which ethical/epistemological framework aligns with which response?

**Variations:**
- Student-designed thought experiments: after studying a theory, students create their own scenario to test it.
- Written response: students write a structured philosophical reflection instead of discussing.

**Avoid when:**
- The scenario is presented without philosophical follow-through (thought experiments aren't just fun puzzles — the analysis matters)
- Students take the scenario too literally instead of engaging with the underlying principle
- Topics hit too close to students' real trauma (e.g. medical dilemmas in a class with recently bereaved students)

## Dilemma Discussion (Dilemma-Diskussion)

**Grades:** 5–S4 (age-appropriate scenarios)
**Phase:** Einstieg, Erarbeitung
**Time:** 20–35 min

**Procedure:**
1. Present a moral dilemma: a situation with two (or more) defensible but conflicting courses of action. No clear "right" answer.
2. Students identify the **values in conflict** (e.g. honesty vs. loyalty, freedom vs. safety).
3. Small groups discuss: What should the person do? Why?
4. Groups present their reasoning. Teacher maps the arguments on the board.
5. Class examines: Which ethical framework supports which position? (Consequentialism? Deontology? Virtue ethics?)
6. Reflection: Is there a resolution, or must we accept the tension?

**Grades 5–6 examples:** a friend asks you to lie to a teacher; you find money that might belong to someone.
**Grades 7–10 examples:** whistleblowing, self-driving car dilemmas, privacy vs. security.
**Oberstufe examples:** euthanasia, distributive justice, civil disobedience — connected to philosophical texts.

**Variations:**
- Kohlberg-style stage analysis: assess the reasoning level of different arguments.
- Dilemma + role play: act out the decision and its consequences.

**Avoid when:**
- One option is obviously correct (no genuine dilemma → no genuine discussion)
- The dilemma is too abstract for the age group (ground it in relatable scenarios)
- No time for proper debriefing (students need closure on emotionally challenging material)

## Opinion Line (Positionslinie / Meinungslinie)

**Grades:** 5–S4
**Phase:** Einstieg, Sicherung
**Time:** 10–15 min

**Procedure:**
1. Mark two poles in the room (e.g. "Strongly Agree" — "Strongly Disagree").
2. Read a provocative statement aloud.
3. Students position themselves physically along the line according to their opinion.
4. Teacher interviews students at various positions: "Why are you standing here?"
5. After hearing arguments, students may reposition.
6. Debrief: What arguments were most persuasive? Did your position change?

**Variations:**
- Four-corner opinion: corners represent distinct positions (not just agree/disagree).
- Silent Opinion Line: students position themselves without speaking, then explain after.
- Written follow-up: students write a paragraph defending their position.

**Avoid when:**
- The topic is so sensitive that students feel exposed by physically showing their opinion
- Room layout doesn't allow movement (use an anonymous digital poll instead)
- Used as a standalone activity without follow-up discussion (the positioning is just the starting point)

## Conceptual Analysis (Begriffsanalyse)

**Grades:** 7–S4
**Phase:** Erarbeitung
**Time:** 20–30 min

**Procedure:**
1. Choose a central concept for analysis (e.g. "freedom", "justice", "knowledge", "happiness", "identity").
2. Students collect **initial definitions** individually: What does this concept mean to you?
3. Collect on the board. Compare: Where do definitions overlap? Where do they diverge?
4. **Test with examples:** Does this definition include cases it shouldn't (too broad)? Does it exclude cases it should include (too narrow)?
5. **Counterexample method:** For each proposed definition, find a counterexample that breaks it.
6. Iterate: refine definitions to handle the counterexamples. Discover that some concepts resist neat definition.
7. Connect to philosophical tradition: How have philosophers defined this concept?

**Variations:**
- Concept map: visualize relationships between the target concept and related concepts.
- Etymology exploration: trace the word's history to uncover layers of meaning.

**Avoid when:**
- The concept is too concrete for meaningful philosophical analysis (e.g. "table" — unless doing Wittgenstein!)
- Students skip the counterexample step (that's where the real philosophical work happens)
- English classes (this method is specifically designed for German Philosophy Unterricht)
