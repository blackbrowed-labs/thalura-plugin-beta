# Text-Based Methods

Methods for reading, analyzing, and working with texts — fundamental to English (literary analysis, reading comprehension) and Philosophy (textual exegesis, argument reconstruction).

---

## Close Reading (Textanalyse)

**Grades:** 7–S4 (simplified annotation for 7–8, full analytical depth from 9)
**Phase:** Erarbeitung
**Time:** 20–40 min

**Procedure:**
1. Select a short passage (1–2 pages max).
2. **1st reading:** content comprehension — what is being said?
3. **2nd reading:** language & style — how is it said? (imagery, tone, word choice, rhetorical devices)
4. **3rd reading:** structure & argument — how is it organized? What is the effect?
5. Students annotate the text (highlighting, margin notes, coding). Compare annotations with a partner.
6. Class discussion of findings, connecting details to overarching themes or arguments.

**Variations:**
- Guided annotation: teacher provides specific codes (e.g. circle metaphors, underline thesis statements).
- Comparative Close Reading: two passages side by side.
- SOAPSTone framework (Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone) for non-fiction.

**Avoid when:**
- Students need a broad overview first (use skimming/scanning before close reading)
- The text is too long for detailed analysis — select a key excerpt
- Grades 5–6 English (reading comprehension is still developing; use simpler guided reading tasks)

## Reading Log (Lesetagebuch)

**Grades:** 5–S4
**Phase:** Begleitend (ongoing alongside reading)
**Time:** 10–15 min per entry (homework or in-class)

**Procedure:**
1. Students keep a notebook/document alongside a longer reading assignment.
2. After each reading session, they write entries: summary, personal reaction, questions, predictions, vocabulary notes, favorite quotes.
3. Teacher provides varied prompts (e.g. "Write a letter to the character", "Draw a scene", "Predict what happens next", "What does this remind you of?").
4. Regular sharing: peer reading of logs, pair discussions, or teacher conferences.
5. Final reflection: how did my understanding change over the course of reading?

**Variations:**
- Digital Reading Log: blog, padlet, or shared document with multimedia.
- Double-entry journal: left column = quote from text, right column = personal response.

**Avoid when:**
- Reading assignment is very short (not enough to sustain a log)
- Entries become mechanical summaries — use creative/reflective prompts
- Students perceive it as busywork (vary the formats, give choice)

## Literature Circles

**Grades:** 7–S4
**Phase:** Erarbeitung
**Time:** 30–45 min per session (runs across multiple sessions)

**Procedure:**
1. Form groups of 4–6. Each member receives a **role card** with specific tasks:
   - **Discussion Director:** prepares open-ended discussion questions
   - **Literary Luminary:** selects important/beautiful passages to read aloud and discuss
   - **Vocabulary Enricher:** identifies and explains key vocabulary
   - **Connector:** links text to real world, other texts, or personal experience
   - **Illustrator:** creates a visual response (drawing, diagram, comic)
2. Students read independently, prepare their role.
3. Group meets and each member contributes from their role (5 min per role).
4. Open discussion follows.
5. Roles rotate with each new reading section.

**Philosophy adaptation:** Replace roles with: Argument Analyst, Real-World Connector, Devil's Advocate, Concept Definer, Historical Context Expert.

**Avoid when:**
- Students can't yet read independently in English at the required level (scaffold with audio support)
- Text is too difficult without teacher guidance (pre-teach key vocabulary and concepts)
- Groups < 3 or > 6 (too few roles, or too many waiting)

## Argument Reconstruction (Argumentrekonstruktion)

**Grades:** 9–S4
**Phase:** Erarbeitung
**Time:** 20–35 min

**Procedure:**
1. Provide a philosophical passage containing an argument.
2. Students identify the **main claim / conclusion**.
3. They work backward to find **supporting premises** (explicit and implicit).
4. They identify **hidden assumptions** the argument relies on.
5. They evaluate **logical validity**: do the premises actually support the conclusion?
6. Results are visualized as **argument maps** (boxes for claims, arrows for support/attack relations) or structured outlines.
7. Class comparison: do different students reconstruct the same argument differently? Why?

**Variations:**
- Toulmin model: claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal.
- Argument mapping software (e.g. MindMup, Argunet).

**Avoid when:**
- The text is narrative rather than argumentative
- Students haven't been introduced to basic logic concepts (teach premise/conclusion distinction first)
- English classes below Oberstufe (the metalanguage of argumentation is demanding)

## Hermeneutical Circle (Hermeneutischer Zirkel)

**Grades:** S1–S4 (requires significant reading maturity)
**Phase:** Erarbeitung, Vertiefung
**Time:** Spans multiple lessons

**Procedure:**
1. **First reading:** global comprehension of the whole text. Note initial understanding.
2. **Close reading of a key passage:** detailed analysis of language, argument, concepts.
3. **Re-read the whole text** with insights from the passage analysis. How does the part illuminate the whole?
4. **Select another passage.** Repeat close reading. Does this change your understanding of the first passage?
5. Students track how their interpretation **evolves** across iterations (written reflection log).
6. Class discussion: compare interpretive trajectories.

**Avoid when:**
- Students encounter the text for the very first time (let them read it fully once first)
- The text is too short for iterative re-reading (< 2 pages)
- Students below Oberstufe (the metacognitive demand of tracking one's own evolving interpretation is high)
- English classes (this is a Philosophy-specific hermeneutical method)
