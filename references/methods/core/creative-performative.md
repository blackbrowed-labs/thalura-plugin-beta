# Creative & Performative Methods

Methods using drama, movement, and creative production — highly engaging for younger learners, valuable for perspective-taking in Philosophy and language production in English.

---

## Standbild / Freeze Frame (Tableau Vivant)

**Grades:** 5–10 (can feel forced with Oberstufe students unless framed carefully)
**Phase:** Erarbeitung, Einstieg
**Time:** 15–25 min

**Procedure:**
1. Small groups receive a scene, concept, or key moment (from a text, ethical dilemma, or abstract idea like "freedom" or "power").
2. Groups create a **frozen image** using their bodies — no movement, no speaking. Each person's posture, gesture, and spatial relationship represents something.
3. Audience observes and interprets: What do you see? What could each figure represent? What relationships are shown?
4. Optional: teacher taps a figure on the shoulder → the figure speaks one sentence "in character" (thought-tracking).
5. Discussion: How does this physical representation deepen understanding of the concept/scene?

**Variations:**
- Before/After Standbild: two images showing a change or development.
- Living sculpture: audience can "rearrange" the figures to show alternative interpretations.

**Avoid when:**
- Students have strong physical self-consciousness (common in puberty, grades 7–9 — offer alternatives)
- Abstract concept is too complex for physical representation
- No space in the classroom for movement

## Role Play & Simulation (Rollenspiel)

**Grades:** 5–S4 (complexity scales with age)
**Phase:** Erarbeitung, Transfer
**Time:** 20–45 min

**Procedure:**
1. Set up the scenario: who are the characters, what's the situation, what's the conflict or task?
2. Assign roles. Provide **role cards** with key information, goals, and language support (especially in English).
3. Preparation time: students plan what they'll say/do (5–10 min).
4. Performance: pairs or small groups act out the scenario.
5. Observation: non-performing students watch with a task (note arguments used, language observed, character development).
6. Debriefing: step out of character first ("I'm no longer X, I'm me again"). Discuss insights.

**English examples:** job interview, town meeting about an issue, characters from a novel meeting, tourist asking for directions.
**Philosophy examples:** ethical committee deciding on a dilemma, historical philosophers debating, court trial on a moral question.

**Variations:**
- Simulation: larger-scale, multi-role scenarios with institutional frameworks (e.g. Model UN, mock trial).
- Improvisation: no preparation — spontaneous role play for fluency practice.

**Avoid when:**
- Students aren't given enough structure (especially lower grades — provide sentence starters, vocabulary lists)
- Sensitive personal topics overlap with assigned roles
- No proper debriefing happens (students stay "in character" emotionally)

## Hot Seat (Heißer Stuhl)

**Grades:** 7–S4
**Phase:** Erarbeitung, Vertiefung
**Time:** 15–25 min

**Procedure:**
1. One student (or the teacher) sits "in character" — as a literary character, historical philosopher, or stakeholder in a dilemma.
2. The class asks questions to the character. The person in the Hot Seat answers **in character**, improvising based on their knowledge.
3. Audience evaluates: Were the answers consistent with the character/thinker? What new insights emerged?
4. Multiple rounds possible with different characters.

**Variations:**
- Double Hot Seat: two characters are questioned simultaneously (e.g. protagonist and antagonist).
- Expert Hot Seat: student presents as an expert on a topic and fields audience questions (no character needed).

**Avoid when:**
- Students haven't studied the character/thinker enough to answer in character
- Only one confident student volunteers (use pair Hot Seat or allow preparation notes)
- The character's views are offensive and students might internalize them without proper framing

## Creative Writing

**Grades:** 5–S4
**Phase:** Erarbeitung, Transfer, Sicherung
**Time:** 20–45 min

**Procedure:**
1. Provide a clear prompt connected to the current topic. Examples:
   - Write an alternative ending to the story.
   - Write a diary entry from Character X's perspective.
   - Compose a philosophical dialogue between two thinkers on [topic].
   - Write a letter from the future to the present about [ethical issue].
2. Pre-writing: brainstorm ideas, create a brief outline.
3. Drafting: silent writing time (15–30 min).
4. Sharing: peer reading in pairs or small groups. Constructive feedback.
5. Optional revision based on feedback.

**Variations:**
- Constrained writing: must include specific vocabulary, rhetorical devices, or argument structures.
- Collaborative writing: each student writes one paragraph, passes to the next.

**Avoid when:**
- Students need analytical rather than creative engagement with the text
- No scaffolding is provided for weaker writers (offer sentence starters, vocabulary banks, model texts)
- Assessment criteria are unclear (creative ≠ anything goes — define expectations)

## Digital Storytelling

**Grades:** 6–S4
**Phase:** Transfer, Sicherung
**Time:** 2–4 lessons (project format)

**Procedure:**
1. Students create a short multimedia narrative (2–5 min) combining: script, voiceover, images/video, music.
2. Planning: storyboard with scenes, narration text, visual ideas.
3. Production: record narration, source/create visuals, edit (tools: iMovie, Adobe Spark, Canva, Book Creator).
4. Screening: class watches all stories. Structured peer feedback.
5. Reflection: how did the medium shape the message?

**Avoid when:**
- Technical infrastructure is unreliable (test devices and software beforehand)
- No time for proper production (half-finished digital stories are frustrating)
- Focus should be on written text quality (digital storytelling shifts attention to media production)
- Students lack basic digital literacy (build skills incrementally)
